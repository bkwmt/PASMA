# Historical Context

<p class="lead">What the paper's networks and embeddings are measuring, and where the account comes from.</p>

Our ISMIR 2026 paper reports a structural break in the backstage collaboration network of Taiwanese popular music around 1978, and reads it against the adoption of multitrack recording, the American military club circuit, the arranger CHEN Chih-yuan 陳志遠, and four arrangement traditions. Six pages could not supply the historical grounding these readings deserve. This page supplies the minimum: each section opens with the sentence from the paper that it grounds, then gives the historical account in a few paragraphs, with sources. It is not a summary of the history itself; that is told in the first author's dissertation [@cheng2026dissertation], from which this page is drawn, together with the oral histories, newspapers and scholarship listed in Section 7.

A note on names. Chinese and Japanese personal names are written surname first, as they are spoken, with the surname in capitals and the characters at first mention: CHEN Chih-yuan 陳志遠. Quotations from the paper, the figures reproduced from it and its labels for the four traditions keep the paper's own spelling (Chen Chih-Yuan).

## 1. Before the studio: a fragmented backstage (1945–1960)

*This section grounds the paper's statements: "the network is visibly fragmented. A main component is organized around several record labels, surrounded by smaller peripheral clusters. The bridging nodes within the main component are predominantly lyricists, while arrangers and engineers occupy marginal positions" and "the Pre-multitrack tradition, before the mid-1970s".*
{: .grounds}

![Figure 2a of the paper: the cumulative collaboration network, 1957–1976. Record labels (diamonds) sit at the centre; lyricists bridge the main component; the Taiwanese-language community stands apart. Circles are persons, diamonds institutions; colour is role (legend below).](assets/img/network_1957_1976.jpg)

![Legend for the network figures.](assets/img/network_legend.png)

Before 1960 there was no studio system to hold the backstage together. Open-air shows, film dubbing, radio and record sessions all existed, but the work behind them followed no repeatable routine. Recording was subordinate to performance: in the early 1950s the composer TSENG Chung-ying 曾仲影 (1922–2021) recorded film music in an actor's tatami room with an Ampex machine and five or six players, no metronome, and a restart from the top after any mistake [@pmdb-oralhistory]. Engineers were attached to sessions, not a profession.

What held the players together was the pre-war record-session habit of a mixed band of five to ten reading at sight and finishing in one pass. KUO I-nan 郭一男 (b. 1931) recalled that the veterans at Asia Records 亞洲唱片 in the late 1950s played the moment a score was handed to them [@pmdb-oralhistory]. This continuity with pre-war East Asian recording practice is what the paper's "Pre-multitrack tradition" refers to [@yamauchi2024phonomod; @cheng2026dissertation].

Arranging, where it was credited at all, was concentrated in one person: LIN Li-han 林禮涵 (1912–1979), a jazz pianist who had joined Victor Records 勝利唱片 in the Japanese era and after the war worked for several labels. A composer handed him the tune; instrumentation, voicing and harmony were his alone, written at the session and conducted in one synchronous take [@new-tsaitunghsiung2003; @new-hungyifeng2006]. This is why the early network looks as it does. Liner notes of the period name a lyricist and composer on every record but an arranger only sometimes, and then almost always Lin; so the lyricists who wrote for several labels bridge the main component, while arrangers and engineers, few and tied to sessions, sit at the margin.

## 2. Media segmentation, language, and label exclusivity (1960–1975)

*This section grounds the paper's statements: "A separate community of Taiwanese-language performers is sparsely linked to the main network, reflecting both cross-label recording prohibitions and state policies prioritizing Mandarin" and that the giant component later absorbed "the previously coexisting production ecologies: Mandarin-language label circuits, the suppressed Taiwanese-language community, and the military-club cohort".*
{: .grounds}

After 1960 radio, records and television became three distinct working regimes. On the front stage they produced clear categories: Mandarin versus Taiwanese, live broadcast versus studio recording, television big band versus nightclub combo. Backstage the same people crossed those lines. The singer CHI Lu-hsia 紀露霞 recalls one-take broadcasts for the Broadcasting Corporation of China, the same musicians moving on to Asia Records to cut several songs in an afternoon, and the tapes going to YEH Ho-ming 葉和鳴, who had built a studio from surplus American equipment, for disc cutting [@pmdb-oralhistory; @cheng2026dissertation].

The market was split by language, and the state stood behind the split. Broadcast time was divided between Mandarin and Taiwanese programmes, and labels ran separate language lines [@黃裕元2016歌唱王國; @chen2017rethinking]. Kuo I-nan, founder of Nan Hsing Records 南星唱片 in Tainan, recalls that the government restricted Taiwanese-language songs on radio and that stations would not say so openly because the Garrison Command monitored them; Tseng Chung-ying recalls the Government Information Office making a television drama he scored switch from Taiwanese to Mandarin in the 1970s [@pmdb-oralhistory]. This is why the Taiwanese-language community in the 1957–1976 snapshot appears sparse and inward-looking.

The paper's phrase "cross-label recording prohibitions" refers to neither a law nor a written contract. Exclusivity was informal and enforced by blacklisting: a singer or musician who recorded for a rival without the home label's blessing would not be hired by that label again. Kuo recounts bringing HUNG Yi-feng 洪一峰 to Asia Records around 1958; after Hung's first hit a Taipei label lured him away and Asia never recorded him again. Tseng, who recorded mainly for one Taipei label in the later 1960s, wrote for others only under pen names [@pmdb-oralhistory]. Loyalty was secured by future access to work, not by a document.

## 3. The American military clubs and the first band wave (1958–1977)

*This section grounds the paper's statements: "A separate cluster emerges, centered on a cohort of pop instrumentalists and their band's keyboardist, who also served as their arranger, all from the American military club circuit, bringing ensemble-playing experience from years of live performance." and "[Chen Chih-Yuan] first appears in the network as the keyboardist of the military-club cohort … and transitioned around 1978 from instrumentalist to commissioned arranger."*
{: .grounds}

![Figure 2b of the paper: 1957–1978. At the threshold a separate cluster of pop instrumentalists from the military-club circuit appears, linked to the main body but not yet absorbed.](assets/img/network_1957_1978.jpg)

Between 1951 and 1979 there were fifteen American military clubs in Taiwan, members-only and graded by rank [@詹孟桐2020美軍]. Against South Korea's more than 270 base clubs, this was a small and closed circuit that never became a wider scene [@shin_ho_2009_translation]. The big-band slots went mostly to professional Filipino musicians; the bandleader CHIN Tsu-ling 金祖齡 wrote in 1968 that ninety per cent of the clubs hired Filipinos, and local bands found their opening where a small combo was wanted [@new-chin1968; @pmdb-oralhistory]. Pay was three to six times an ordinary wage, and the entry condition was measurable: forty to fifty songs in stock, because a night meant four hours of continuous playing [@shin_ho_2009_translation; @pmdb-oralhistory].

The clubs were not the cradle of Taiwanese pop that later accounts make them. Club musicians had almost no chance to enter the record industry, where labels preferred to reproduce "original" recordings [@shin_ho_2009_translation]. What the clubs did transmit was a work logic different from radio, records and television, where the singer or programme was the centre: in the clubs the band's overall sound was the unit of work, and years of four-hour nights built the ensemble coordination that records and books could not [@cheng2026dissertation]. The Vietnam War enlarged the circuit; the guitarist YU Cheng-yen 游正彥 recalls Taichung filling with servicemen, clubs needing more bands than there were musicians, and off-duty Americans who had been professionals at home sitting in [@pmdb-oralhistory].

The cohort behind the separate cluster in the 1957–1978 snapshot is the younger generation of this wave: Yu Cheng-yen, the drummer HUANG Jui-feng 黃瑞豐 and the bassist KUO Tsung-shao 郭宗韶, all born around 1950, who entered the clubs just as the position of studio musician was forming and became its first occupants [@cheng2026dissertation]. Admission to the clubs ran by recognition from a senior player followed by personal introduction; the same mechanism reappears in the studios after 1979.

## 4. Multitrack recording and the Platinum studio (1977–1980)

*This section grounds the paper's statements: "the industrial adoption and standardization of multitrack studio workflows in the late 1970s"; "1976–1978 is not an arbitrary periodization, but a genuine structural threshold coinciding with the introduction and industrial adoption of multitrack recording in Taiwan"; the 1957–1991 snapshot in which "Recording studios, absent from the earlier graphs, now occupy the center alongside engineers and multiple arrangers … Record labels … have migrated to the periphery"; and the reading of engineer enrichment as early "scarcity, just a few individuals bridging the entire network" followed by later "diffusion".*
{: .grounds}

![Three structural metrics on five-year sliding windows (one-year step): the average clustering coefficient, the largest-component ratio and Louvain modularity turn together in 1976–1978.](assets/img/temporal_curves.png)

The case is Platinum Studio 白金錄音室 in Sanchong, Taipei, because its documentation is the most complete; Lifeng 麗風, A String 雅絃, Rhythm 捷奏, Good Sound 佳聲 and Mega Force 強力 operated in the same years [@cheng2026dissertation]. The push came from the cutting lathe. YEH Chui-ching 葉垂青 (b. 1952), whose family owned First Records 第一唱片, installed Taiwan's first computer-controlled lathe, a Neumann VMS70, in 1977 and cut nearly every LP master in the country until the CD era. Tapes arrived with levels and balance varying from song to song, and he concluded that the problem had to be fixed upstream, in the recording itself [@程明2021聲響技術, pp. 71–72; @cheng-fieldwork, interviews from 2015].

The Yehs had met the Japanese engineer HIRATA Giichi 平田義一 (d. 2019) at a European audio fair in 1973, and he advised them to build a studio [@程明2021聲響技術, p. 72]. At the end of 1978 they ran an experimental session: 52 Taipei session players assembled through HSU Chung-hsien 徐崇憲 of Lifeng, Japanese arrangers and engineers brought by Hirata, and a borrowed 16-track machine with a Neve console. The resulting album, *Changcheng Yao* 長城謠 (First Records, 1978), advertised both the largest orchestra then recorded in Taiwan and multitrack recording; it sold well, and the Yehs committed about NT$30 million to Platinum, which Hirata reported in 1979 as the island's first 24-track studio [@程明2021聲響技術, pp. 74–75; @hirata_japan_1979].

Multitrack did not arrive with Platinum. Hsu had bought an 8-track system for Lifeng in 1976, and Hirata counted three 16-track studios in 1979 [@hirata_japan_1979]. What 1976–1980 fixed was a configuration: Hirata's brief for Platinum (isolated rooms with an acoustic lock, 24 tracks as the top standard, acoustic measurement at each construction stage) became the uniform look of Taipei studios in the 1980s, and other owners had to upgrade or lose work [@hirata_1998_baijin; @cheng-fieldwork, 2018]. This is closure in the sense of the Social Construction of Technology: the equipment stabilized when an arrangement of personnel, tacit knowledge and labour rhythm formed around it, and what shifted underneath was the criterion of a good record, from a full orchestra playing together to efficiency under the dense release schedule of the campus folk-song movement [@Bijker1987FactsAndArtefacts; @cheng2026dissertation]. Socially, multitrack means that players learn to play to a click track, arrangers plan the song in recordable sections, and engineers judge single tracks without hearing the ensemble [@horning2013chasing].

Two further features of the 1957–1991 graph follow. Studios sit at the centre because none of Taipei's nearly two hundred record and production companies owned one; recording, mixing and mastering were outsourced to commercial studios, which therefore held the best equipment and the busiest schedules [@hirata_1998_baijin, p. 78; @theberge2004network]. And engineers pass from scarcity to diffusion: WANG Chia-tung 王家棟 recalls checking LP credits in a Taipei record shop in 1983 and finding Hsu Chung-hsien's name on an estimated nine tenths of them; from about 1986 the work spread to Platinum and other rooms [@pmdb-oralhistory].

## 5. CHEN Chih-yuan and the segmented arrangement (1978–1991)

*This section grounds the paper's statements: "The single most powerful bridge in the entire 1957–1991 network is the arranger designated as Arr. #1 … Chen Chih-Yuan. Chen first appears in the network as the keyboardist of the military-club cohort, and transitioned around 1978 from instrumentalist to commissioned arranger. By the end of the period, he was active across virtually the entire industry: every major label, studio, and stylistic tradition in the dataset" (4.2) and "the works by Chen Chih-Yuan and his protégé are spread across the entire space, overlapping with every other tradition … the person who crossed all traditions also produced sound that crosses all clusters" (4.3), together with Table 1's observation that arrangers, pop instrumentalists, backing vocalists and producers take over the bridging positions after 1978.*
{: .grounds}

![Figure 2c of the paper: 1957–1991. One giant component. Recording studios sit at the centre with engineers and several arrangers; labels and lyricists are pushed to the edge. Arr. #1 is CHEN Chih-yuan.](assets/img/network_1957_1991.jpg)

The arranger's rise was an effect of production volume. The first Golden Melody Prize 金韻獎 campus contest in 1977 and its compilation series, Haishan's 海山 annual *Folk Style* 民謠風 from 1978, and the founding of Rock Records 滾石 (1981) and UFO 飛碟 (1982) institutionalized large-scale production in the Mandarin record industry [@張釗維1994誰在那邊; @何東洪張釗維2000國語唱片]. The work went to commercial studios that had to keep rooms, engineers and session players busy; Wang Chia-tung recalled running Lifeng around the clock for two months in 1983 with Hsu Chung-hsien as its only other engineer [@new-tmcia-bandfire36].

Pressure alone did not pick a workflow. The one that won is what the trade calls the segmented arrangement 分段編曲, the working method of CHEN Chih-yuan 陳志遠 (1950–2011): on receiving a demo he fixed form, style and harmony, handed the rhythm-section parts over for immediate recording, and wrote the strings while that session ran, for recording the next day [@程明2021聲響技術]. Once 24-track machines were standard, the equipment still admitted two readings, a tool for patching synchronous takes or a condition for splitting production into stages; the second stabilized between roughly 1977 and 1981 because folk-era volume, the need for an arranger, the rhythm section's tacit coordination and the entry of the producer title all pushed the same way [@cheng2026dissertation]. Its material carrier was Chen's one-page master score 總譜, with every instrumental line on a single sheet, which became the entry threshold for studio work [@王啟仲2022重探臺灣搖滾樂場景中]. The producer LI Tsung-sheng 李宗盛 stated that Chen carried the largest arranging volume in the industry, charging NT$4,000 a song while clients queued past midnight [@王淳眉2016製作]. Solos and timbral details were deliberately left to particular players, a "structural blank" 結構性留白 described from the players' side in the oral histories [@pmdb-oralhistory].

Chen came to this position from the keyboard. With Huang Jui-feng, Yu Cheng-yen and Kuo Tsung-shao he formed the rhythm section known as the Gang of Four 四人幫 [@馬世芳2015時空地圖; @chu2020rnr]; he is less often remembered as a club musician than the other three, because his later role as arranger eclipsed his years as a band keyboardist, which is why the paper counts him with the military-club cohort. The keyboard is the one band position that handles harmony, voicing and form at once, so when studios needed someone to fix form, harmony and key before recording, the keyboardist's skill was the easiest to enlarge into the arranger's post [@cheng2026dissertation]. The centrality we measure is therefore a structural trace, not evidence of a lone genius: around 1977 studio time, players, producer expectations and label caseloads all concentrated on the arranger, and Chen was the person best able to bear it. He recalled seventy-two hours without sleep [@pmdb-oralhistory].

The producer 製作人 title was still unfamiliar around 1980; LI Shou-chuan 李壽全 recalls that when he asked Chen for a cello on *Descendants of the Dragon* (1980), Chen told him it was the first time a producer had instructed him how to arrange [@pmdb-oralhistory]. By the mid-1980s producer intervention in arrangement was a convention [@becker2008artworlds]. Chen's move into UFO as an in-house producer in 1987, after which he generally declined other labels' work, is why his brokerage rank drops from first in the 1982–1986 window to eighteenth in 1987–1991, with the bridging positions passing to studios, backing vocalists, session players and engineers [@cheng2026dissertation].

## 6. Four arrangement traditions

*This section grounds the paper's definition: "we defined four arrangement traditions: (1) the Pre-multitrack tradition, before the mid-1970s; (2) the Chen Chih-Yuan tradition, covering Chen and his protégé; (3) the Electone tradition, a parallel path rooted in the electronic organ, serving primarily the Taiwanese-language market under tighter budget constraints; and (4) the Post-Chen era, styles from the late 1980s onward." We also wrote that "the Pre-multitrack tradition, the Electone tradition, and the Post-Chen era each occupy distinct regions of the embedding space."*
{: .grounds}

![Figure 3b of the paper: UMAP projection of MERT embeddings of vocal-separated recordings, coloured by arrangement tradition. Each point is a recording.](assets/img/mert_traditions.png)

**Pre-multitrack tradition.** Arrangers who worked before multitrack was standardized, when a band recorded together and arranging was coupled to conducting. The MERT sample represents it with Lin Li-han, HSU Shih 許石 and WENG Ching-hsi 翁清溪, big-band arrangers of the generation described in Section 1 [@cheng2026dissertation].

**Chen Chih-Yuan tradition.** The Mandarin core arranging position at the moment of closure around 1979, represented by Chen and his protégé CHEN Yu-li 陳玉立, the keyboardist who stood in for him when he was overbooked. "Protégé" is used in the loose sense the Taiwanese trade gives it: a younger musician who takes over some of a senior's cases is spoken of as that senior's student, and the senior is addressed as 老師 (teacher), without any formal apprenticeship [@cheng2026dissertation].

**Electone tradition.** The paper's label corresponds to the Taiwanese-language electronic-organ arranging circle 臺語電子琴編曲圈, defined by production conditions rather than language alone: lower budgets, the electronic organ as lead instrument, and a working milieu separate from the Mandarin core studios. The MERT sample uses LIN Kuo-hsiung 林國雄, LIU Ching-chih 劉清池 (b. 1943) and CHI Ming-yang 紀明陽. Liu's career shows why this is a parallel path and not a margin: he arranged in both languages until the industry split into Taiwanese and Mandarin camps after the folk-song wave, when Lin Li-han handed him the Taiwanese-language work; by number of songs arranged he ranks first in Taiwanese popular music history [@new-linmiaozi2024; @chen2022decolonization]. The circle's MIDI keyboards were called 魔音琴 ("magic-sound keyboard") and its sound 俗擱有力 (Taiwanese: "vulgar yet powerful"), a stance the circle took towards the same tools the Mandarin core absorbed into a centralized workflow [@chen2022decolonization; @cheng2026dissertation]. It was never absorbed, and the UMAP projections indicate a separate region, with and without Chen's works in the input.

**Post-Chen era.** Arrangers who entered the Mandarin core position only after the Chen model had closed around it: TU Hui-yuan 凃惠源, TU Ying 屠穎 (1963–2025), WANG Yu-min 王豫民, HUANG Yun-ling 黃韻玲 and CHUNG Hsing-min 鍾興民, five chosen for balance rather than as a roster. "Post-Chen" is the analytical label; the cohort called itself "MIDI Boy" [@cheng2026dissertation]. All are keyboard-trained, continuing the lineage of Section 5 [@何佳娜2017默會]. What changed was access: Tu Ying recalls that roughly ten younger arrangers got their chance only because of MIDI, and that knowledge of Chen's method travelled indirectly, by listening to his Platinum multitracks one track at a time with an engineer [@pmdb-oralhistory]. The MERT projections suggest that this cohort's works occupy a region distinct from both the pre-multitrack and Electone groups while overlapping with Chen's, and that removing Chen's works sharpens the separation.

## 7. Sources and how to read them

**The dissertation.** Cheng (2026) is a history of the recording studio as a social institution, written from the position of a working session guitarist and framed by the Social Construction of Technology and Antoine Hennion's sociology of amateurs [@cheng2026dissertation]. Chapters 3 to 5 cover the periods on this page. It is written in Chinese.

**Oral histories.** Most first-person testimony comes from the *Oral History of Senior Musicians* collected since 2010 by the Taipei Music Center's Taiwan Popular Music Database [@pmdb-oralhistory], more than a hundred filmed interviews. They are cited collectively here, with the interviewee named in the sentence; testimony is paraphrased, never quoted verbatim. The first author's own field interviews (2015–2020) are cited as [@cheng-fieldwork].

**Newspapers, trade magazines and scholarship.** The *United Daily News* 聯合報 and *Economic Daily News* 經濟日報 supply dated evidence for the 1950s and 1960s; Japanese audio-engineering journals and *Studio Sound* record Hirata Giichi's reports on Taiwan's studios. For the Taiwanese-language record industry, HUANG Yu-yuan [@黃裕元2016歌唱王國] and CHEN Pei-feng [@chen2017rethinking; @chen2022decolonization]; for the military clubs, CHAN Meng-tung [@詹孟桐2020美軍], Shin and Ho [@shin_ho_2009_translation] and Chu [@chu2020rnr]; for arrangers' tacit knowledge, HO Chia-na [@何佳娜2017默會]; for the studio as a technology, Horning [@horning2013chasing] and Théberge [@theberge2004network].
