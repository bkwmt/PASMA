# Historical Context

<p class="lead">What the paper's networks and embeddings are measuring, in the words of the people who made the records.</p>

Our ISMIR 2026 paper reports a structural break in the backstage collaboration network of Taiwanese popular music around 1978, and reads it against the industrial adoption of multitrack recording, the American military club circuit, the arranger Chen Chih-Yuan 陳志遠, and four arrangement traditions. Six pages could not supply the historical and bibliographic grounding these readings deserve. This page supplies it. Each section opens with the sentence from the paper that it grounds, then gives the historical account and the sources.

The account is drawn from the first author's doctoral dissertation, *The Closure and Loosening of the Recording Studio: Behind-the-Scenes as Method for Narrating Postwar Taiwanese Popular Music History* (National Taiwan University, 2026, in Chinese) [@cheng2026dissertation], and from the sources it assembles: the Taipei Music Center's oral-history archive of senior musicians, contemporary newspapers and trade magazines, Japanese audio-engineering journals, and the Taiwanese and English scholarship listed in the references. Oral testimony is paraphrased, never quoted verbatim. Personal names are given in the romanization conventional in Taiwan, with Chinese characters at first mention. Multitrack recording is described throughout as *adopted and standardized*, because, as Section 4 shows, the equipment preceded the workflow by several years.

## 1. Before the studio: a fragmented backstage (1945–1960)

*This section grounds the paper's statements: "the network is visibly fragmented. A main component is organized around several record labels, surrounded by smaller peripheral clusters. The bridging nodes within the main component are predominantly lyricists, while arrangers and engineers occupy marginal positions" and "the Pre-multitrack tradition, before the mid-1970s".*
{: .grounds}

![Figure 2a of the paper: the cumulative collaboration network, 1957–1976. Record labels (diamonds) sit at the centre; lyricists bridge the main component; the Taiwanese-language community stands apart. Circles are persons, diamonds institutions; colour is role (legend below).](assets/img/network_1957_1976.jpg)

![Legend for the network figures.](assets/img/network_legend.png)


The first author (Cheng 2026) argues that before 1980 the backstage (幕後, the lyricists, composers, arrangers, session players and engineers behind a recording) was not yet held together by a stable "studio system", and that 1945–1960 shows this most clearly. Open-air shows, film dubbing, radio and record sessions all existed, but the work behind them followed no repeatable routine; musicians relied on their own training, on people they could call at short notice, and on whatever equipment was at hand. [@cheng2026dissertation]

### Music on the grass

The dissertation borrows Huang Yu-yuan's 黃裕元 phrase "music on the grass" (草地上的音樂) for this state: temporary, mobile, not institutionalized. [@黃裕元2016歌唱王國] In 1946 Hung Yi-feng 洪一峰 (1927–2010) and Cheng Jih-ching 鄭日清 (1924–2014) ran an open-air stage on the Tamsui riverbank, Hung playing, singing and running the programme while selling song booklets by day; in 1947 the Tokyo-trained composer Hsu Shih 許石 (b. 1919) went to Hengchun with Wen Hsia 文夏 (1928–2022) to collect folk tunes as material. [@pmdb-oralhistory] Recording was not absent, but it was not a separate profession. In the early 1950s Tseng Chung-ying 曾仲影 (1922–2021) recorded film music in the tatami room of actor Wu Fei-sung 吳非宋, with an Ampex tape machine and five or six players on piano, accordion, saxophone and bass, no metronome, and a restart from the top after any mistake; he recalled that recording quality was not his concern, the band was. [@pmdb-oralhistory] Recording was subordinate to performance; engineers of this period were attached to sessions, not a distinct group.

### Two streams, many lineages

Contemporary journalism sorted postwar musicians into two streams, Japanese-era holdovers and mainland arrivals, as in Lü Su-shang's 呂訴上 1954 survey of early Taiwanese records and Peng Hung-hsing's 彭虹星 1955 retrospective. [@new-lusushang1954; @new-penghungxing1955] The dissertation treats this as a front-stage classification: each stream was internally plural. [@cheng2026dissertation] The Japanese-era side combined conservatory-trained players returned from Tokyo, church and school musicians, and Han-instrument players from gezaixi (歌仔戲, Taiwanese opera) and nanguan/beiguan ensembles. What let them work together was the pre-war record-session habit of five to ten mixed players reading at sight and finishing in one pass; Kuo I-nan 郭一男 (b. 1931) recalled that the veteran players in Chuang Chi-sheng's 莊啟勝 band at Asia Records 亞洲唱片 in the late 1950s played the moment a score was handed to them. [@pmdb-oralhistory] This is the continuity with pre-war East Asian recording practice that the paper's "Pre-multitrack tradition" refers to. [@yamauchi2024phonomod]

The mainland side brought shidaiqu (時代曲, Shanghai popular song), film songs and ballroom jazz, but as individuals rather than as transplanted ensembles. [@黃奇智2014時代曲; @葉月瑜2000歌聲魅影] Tseng Chung-ying, born in Shanghai, came as a radio announcer, was detained for two years after 1947, then played trumpet and clarinet in troupes and gezaixi companies and learned arranging by American correspondence course. [@pmdb-oralhistory] Kuan Hua-shih 關華石 (b. 1912) began with Cantonese music, studied violin in Shanghai and led the all-Chinese Chung-hua Big Band 中華大樂隊 in Shanghai ballrooms; he reached Taiwan in 1949 with a bedroll, a suitcase and two violins. [@孔雅玄2020正聲; @許書維2019群星會] The supply side was therefore a multi-source network rather than two opposed blocs.

### Film dubbing and the big band

Shih Chung's 石鐘 1955 column on film dubbing describes producers looking for musicians only when the release date was announced, then working eight or nine players through the night; he noted that "actors get ten or twenty thousand, players one or two hundred". [@new-shihchung1955a] Collaboration existed, but without fixed posts, workflow or pay. Ko Hsiung-chang's 柯雄昌 1956 article presented Hollywood scoring vocabulary at the same moment: the professional standard was available as reference before it was practised. [@new-kohsiungchang1956] A different form was the Kupa Big Band 鼓霸樂隊, founded in 1953 by Taiwanese player Hsieh Teng-hui 謝騰輝, who brought instruments and scores back from Japan and modelled the group on "the latest American jazz band" formation; the 1956 report stresses that it was an association of enthusiasts with no contracts and no reliable funding. [@new-lianhebao1956kupa] New formats travelled through amateur networks before any market supported them.

### The single arranger

Within these scenes, arranging became an identifiable role, concentrated in Lin Li-han 林禮涵 (1912–1979), a jazz pianist and violinist who had joined Victor Records 勝利唱片 in the Japanese era and after the war worked for several labels from his base in Sanchong. Where a record of the period names an arranger at all, it is almost always Lin. [@new-tsaitunghsiung2003] A composer handed him the tune; instrumentation, voicing and harmony were his decision alone; he wrote the parts at the session and conducted the band in one synchronous take. Hung Yi-feng recalled that he could arrange "only a dozen or so sides at a time, like sprouting beans". [@new-hungyifeng2006] This single-arranger, big-band, one-pass mode is the state that the later standardization and the Chen Chih-Yuan 陳志遠 model overturn once multitrack recording was adopted. [@cheng2026dissertation] Liu Ching-chih 劉清池 came up in Lin's band, and Weng Ching-hsi 翁清溪 (1936–2012) belongs to the same generation of big-band arrangers; training ran through such working ties, not schools. This concentration is one reason arrangers appear as few, marginal nodes in the early network: one person absorbed most of the credited work.

From 1955 the expanding Taiwanese-language record trade demanded periodic releases and a steady supply of masters, and the ad hoc mode began to show its limits. [@cheng2026dissertation]

## 2. Media segmentation, language, and label exclusivity (1960–1975)

*This section grounds the paper's statements: "A separate community of Taiwanese-language performers is sparsely linked to the main network, reflecting both cross-label recording prohibitions and state policies prioritizing Mandarin" and that the giant component later absorbed "the previously coexisting production ecologies: Mandarin-language label circuits, the suppressed Taiwanese-language community, and the military-club cohort".*
{: .grounds}

### Three media, three sets of working conditions

After 1960, the first author (Cheng 2026) argues, radio, records and television became three distinct working regimes for backstage personnel. Radio needed live, stable accompaniment to fill a daily schedule; records needed a performance that could be cut, pressed and sold; television needed large bands fitted to the camera and a star system. On the frontstage these regimes produced clear categories: Mandarin versus Taiwanese, radio live versus studio recording, television big band versus nightclub combo [@cheng2026dissertation].

Backstage, the same people crossed those lines. The singer Chi Lu-hsia 紀露霞, who sang live for the Broadcasting Corporation of China (BCC 中國廣播公司) in the 1950s and 1960s, recalls one-take broadcasts with band, singer and announcer in the same room, the same musicians moving on to Asia Records 亞洲唱片 to cut several songs in an afternoon, and the tapes going to Yeh Ho-ming 葉和鳴 for disc cutting [@pmdb-oralhistory]. Yeh, who worked at the United States Information Service in Taipei and built the Ho-ming studio 和鳴錄音室 from surplus American equipment, is the technical link between broadcast, record and pressing plant [@cheng2026dissertation].

For the record industry the dissertation uses two newspaper reports. In 1967 the Economic Daily News described an industry that had moved from a postwar "vacuum" and underground reprinting to pressing plants and studios after 1952, with a provincial record manufacturers' association founded in 1960 [@new-zhou1967]. In 1968 a report on the opening of Yi Sheng 藝聲錄音唱片公司 in Kaohsiung advertised six presses, a sixteen-piece house band and a dual Taiwanese and Mandarin line [@new-lin1968]. The first author treats the figures as self-promotion but reads the dual-language plan as a sign of a market split by language.

Television began in 1962 with Taiwan Television (TTV 臺灣電視公司). Its variety show Qunxinghui 群星會 started with a small combo; by 1971 the Kupa Big Band 鼓霸大樂隊 had contracted with TTV to form the fifteen-piece TTV Big Band 臺視大樂團, whose deputy leader Yang Shui-chin 楊水金 (b. 1939) recalls four trumpets, four trombones, four saxophones and a doubled rhythm section [@pmdb-oralhistory]. Television strengthened the frontstage image of the Mandarin star [@cheng2026dissertation; @許書維2019群星會]. The bridges between media were arrangers such as Huang Jen-ching 黃仁清 (b. 1937), who joined Li Ko Records 麗歌唱片 in 1967 and arranged Taiwanese songs, Mandarin songs and television themes alike, and Yang himself, who carried jazz harmony learned from Filipino musicians in Taipei dance halls into television and record work [@cheng2026dissertation].

### A market split by language, and the state behind it

The dissertation treats the binaries of postwar music history (Mandarin and Taiwanese, mainlander and islander) as frontstage categories that were nonetheless real: they correspond to political classification, broadcast time slots divided between Mandarin and Taiwanese programmes, and record companies' separate language lines, a picture already present in 1950s newspaper surveys [@cheng2026dissertation; @new-lusushang1954; @new-penghungxing1955]. The record market felt this most directly: Taiwanese records, Mandarin records and cover versions were kept apart by label, star image, printed song booklets (koa-á-chheh 歌仔冊) and regional touring. For the Taiwanese-language industry the first author relies on Huang Yu-yuan's history of Taiwanese-language song, 1945–1971 [@黃裕元2016歌唱王國], and on Chen Pei-feng's studies of the same repertoire under martial law [@chen2017rethinking; @chen2022decolonization].

The dissertation does not analyse language policy itself; it supplies testimony about how the policy was felt. Kuo I-nan 郭一男 (b. c. 1932), founder of Nan Hsing Records 南星唱片 in Tainan, recalls that the government restricted Taiwanese-language songs on radio and that stations would not say so openly because the Taiwan Garrison Command 警備總司令部 monitored them. His pupil Lung Piao-piao 龍飄飄 moved to Mandarin after her brother, a radio announcer, learned of the restriction; Kuo stopped making records and from 1974 took a choir to Japan instead [@pmdb-oralhistory]. The composer Tseng Chung-ying 曾仲影 (1922–2021) recalls that in the 1970s the Government Information Office (GIO 新聞局) made a Chinese Television System (CTS 中華電視) drama he scored, Wang ni zao gui 望你早歸, switch from Taiwanese to Mandarin, and that actors who could not speak Mandarin were tested [@pmdb-oralhistory].

### Exclusivity without contracts

The paper's phrase "cross-label recording prohibitions" refers to neither a law nor a written contract. The first author's gloss is that exclusivity in this period was informal and enforced by blacklisting: a singer or musician who recorded for a rival label without the home label's blessing would not be hired by that label again. Two interviews describe the mechanism. Kuo I-nan recounts bringing Hung Yi-feng 洪一峰 from Taipei to Asia Records around 1958–1962; after Hung's first hit a Taipei label lured him away, Asia never recorded him again, and Kuo says he had warned Hung of exactly that [@pmdb-oralhistory]. Asia's promised session for Kuo's pupil Wang Hsiu-ju 王秀如 lapsed at the same time, which is why Kuo founded Nan Hsing. Tseng Chung-ying, who recorded mainly for the Taipei label Huanchiu 環球唱片 (not the multinational) in the later 1960s, wrote for other labels only under the pen names Tung-fang Pai 東方白 and Yü Niu 余牛, because Huanchiu had treated him well and he would not put his own name on a rival's record [@pmdb-oralhistory]. In both cases loyalty is secured by future access to work, not by a signed document.

### Reading the snapshot

This is the setting of the paper's 1957–1976 snapshot. Singers in the Taiwanese-language sector were held in place by informal exclusivity and lost their radio outlet, so the community appears sparse and inward-looking; the links that do exist run through the arrangers, engineers and session musicians the dissertation calls horizontal nodes [@cheng2026dissertation]. Of the three ecologies named in Section 4.1, the Mandarin label circuits and the Taiwanese-language community are described here; the military-club cohort is treated in the dissertation's next section (US military clubs and the pre-folk campus scene, 1958–1977) and in the corresponding section of this page.

## 3. The American military clubs and the first band wave (1958–1977)

*This section grounds the paper's statements: "A separate cluster emerges, centered on a cohort of pop instrumentalists and their band's keyboardist, who also served as their arranger, all from the American military club circuit, bringing ensemble-playing experience from years of live performance." and "[Chen Chih-Yuan] first appears in the network as the keyboardist of the military-club cohort … and transitioned around 1978 from instrumentalist to commissioned arranger."*
{: .grounds}

![Figure 2b of the paper: 1957–1978. At the threshold a separate cluster of pop instrumentalists from the military-club circuit appears, linked to the main body but not yet absorbed.](assets/img/network_1957_1978.jpg)


### A small and closed circuit

After the Sino-American Mutual Defense Treaty took effect in the mid-1950s, US forces in Taiwan needed live bands playing American dance music, jazz and pop in Taipei (Yuanshan 圓山), Taichung (Ching Chuan Kang air base 清泉崗) and Tainan 臺南. Between 1951 and 1979 there were 15 American military clubs in all of Taiwan (6 in Taipei, 6 in Taichung, 1 in Tainan, 2 in Kaohsiung). Each was strictly members-only and graded by rank; local people other than club employees could hardly enter [@詹孟桐2020美軍]. Shin and Ho contrast this with South Korea's more than 270 base clubs and institutionalized camp show scene: Taiwan's clubs never formed a wider scene, though they offered an alternative path beside Mandarin pop [@shin_ho_2009_translation]. The first author (Cheng 2026) follows this judgment: a scene of limited size, closed form and low public visibility [@cheng2026dissertation].

The big-band slots went mostly to professional Filipino musicians. A 1969 newspaper notice for a "Pop Music Concert" at Zhongshan Hall 中山堂 lists "the Filipino big band of the Linkou club 林口俱樂部" alongside eight local pop bands [@new-lianhebao1969]. In 1968 the bandleader Chin Tsu-ling 金祖齡 (1937–2014) wrote in a magazine special that ninety per cent of the American clubs in Taiwan hired Filipino musicians, "truly a great loss for Taiwan's musicians" [@new-chin1968]. Local bands found their opening where a small ensemble was wanted: Chin recalled that his five-piece Marlboro 馬波羅合唱團 entered the Navy club on Zhongshan North Road in the early 1960s for that reason, with a Filipino saxophonist among the five [@pmdb-oralhistory].

### Pay, repertoire and stamina

Money was the main incentive. Shin and Ho estimate that club musicians in the 1960s earned three to six times an ordinary wage, and that local musicians therefore imitated American music for a limited audience rather than developing their own work [@shin_ho_2009_translation]. Chin's own figures show a steeper spread at the top: NT$20,000 per member at the Linkou club in 1967, against NT$3,000 for the band Telstar 電星合唱團 playing a hotel at the same time. The clubs also set a measurable entry condition: a band needed forty to fifty songs in stock, because a night meant four hours of continuous playing [@pmdb-oralhistory].

### What the clubs did and did not transmit

The dissertation corrects the received image of the clubs as the cradle of Taiwanese pop. Shin and Ho note that club musicians with strong playing skills had almost no chance to enter the record industry, since labels preferred to reproduce "original" recordings [@shin_ho_2009_translation]. The first author argues that the clubs' most direct effect on the record industry was material: Asia Records 亞洲唱片, founded in 1957, bought its first recording equipment from US military surplus at a second-hand shop in Dalin, Chiayi 嘉義大林 [@楊清雅2016呂金守]. Local musicians' command of Anglo-American styles came mainly through radio, pirated records, local nightclubs, instrument shops and peer imitation, not through the club stage [@cheng2026dissertation].

What the clubs did provide was a work logic different from radio, records and television, where the singer or program was the center and instrumentalists sat in accompaniment. In the clubs the band's overall sound was the unit of work: repertoire, instrumentation, groove, volume control and response to the room were all part of the job [@cheng2026dissertation]. Individual skills travelled the same way: Yang Shui-chin 楊水金 learned jazz harmony from a New York guitarist on the nightclub circuit [@pmdb-oralhistory], Hsu Chih-min 許治民 (c. 1947–) learned string bending by watching a Filipino guitarist's left hand [@cheng2026dissertation], and Kuo Tsung-shao 郭宗韶 was pulled onto bass at the Night Paris 夜巴黎 dance hall because no one else understood the instrument [@new-kuo2001].

### The first band wave and its cohort

In chapter 5 the first author distinguishes three band waves and places the first in the mid-1960s to early 1970s, centered on the clubs [@cheng2026dissertation]. The Vietnam War enlarged it. The guitarist Yu Cheng-yen 游正彥 recalls that with a US base at Ching Chuan Kang, Taichung filled with servicemen, clubs needed more bands than there were musicians, and players were pushed up faster than usual; and that enlisted Americans who had been professional musicians at home brought their guitars from the barracks, so that a five-piece stage often ended with eight or nine players jamming [@pmdb-oralhistory]. The first author locates here the formation of fingering, ensemble coordination and a shared sense of sound, which records and books could not transmit one way [@cheng2026dissertation]. Entry was selective: Yu remembers that only top players got into the clubs, and that a band leader who had heard him play recruited him into Ching Chuan Kang [@pmdb-oralhistory].

The wave had two generations. The older one is documented in the 1968 special issue of Today Pictorial 今天畫刊 (written largely by the musicians and radio hosts themselves) and includes Chin Tsu-ling, active in clubs and hotels from the late 1950s; this generation entered the record industry, if at all, before standardization, mostly as composers, producers or go-betweens [@new-jintianhuakan1968; @熊信淵2017熱門; @cheng2026dissertation]. The same issue records the ceiling: local bands were cut short by two-year conscription, under headlines such as "Telstar to disband" [@new-lo1968]. Weng Hsiao-liang 翁孝良 (1945–) turned professional in 1969, played lead guitar in Telstar from about 1971 to 1979, then entered the record industry as composer and producer [@new-weng2015; @pmdb-oralhistory]. The younger generation, Yu Cheng-yen, Huang Jui-feng 黃瑞豐 and Kuo Tsung-shao (all born around 1950), entered the same venues just as the position of studio musician was forming in the standardization of the late 1970s, and became its first occupants [@cheng2026dissertation]. This is the cohort behind the separate cluster in our 1957–1978 snapshot. The dissertation adds that recognition by a senior player, followed by personal introduction, was already the admission mechanism in the clubs; it reappears in the studio after multitrack recording was standardized around 1979 [@cheng2026dissertation].

## 4. Multitrack recording and the Platinum studio (1977–1980)

*This section grounds the paper's statements: "the industrial adoption and standardization of multitrack studio workflows in the late 1970s"; "1976–1978 is not an arbitrary periodization, but a genuine structural threshold coinciding with the introduction and industrial adoption of multitrack recording in Taiwan"; the 1957–1991 snapshot in which "Recording studios, absent from the earlier graphs, now occupy the center alongside engineers and multiple arrangers … Record labels … have migrated to the periphery"; and the reading of engineer enrichment as early "scarcity, just a few individuals bridging the entire network" followed by later "diffusion".*
{: .grounds}

![Three structural metrics on five-year sliding windows (one-year step): the average clustering coefficient, the largest-component ratio and Louvain modularity turn together in 1976–1978.](assets/img/temporal_curves.png)


The account follows chapter 4 of the first author's dissertation (Cheng 2026), which takes Platinum Studio 白金錄音室 in Sanchong 三重, Taipei, as its case while stressing that Lifeng Studio 麗風錄音室, A String 雅絃, Rhythm 捷奏, Good Sound 佳聲 and Mega Force 強力 operated in the same years; Platinum is the case because its documentation and oral sources are the most complete [@cheng2026dissertation].

### 4.1 The push came from the cutting lathe

Yeh Chin-tai 葉進泰 (1928–2019), an electrical engineering graduate and plastics manufacturer, co-founded Ming Feng Records 鳴鳳唱片 in 1957, founded First Records 第一唱片 and, in 1988, the optical-disc maker Ritek 錸德 [@cheng2026dissertation; @pmdb-oralhistory]. Andrew F. Jones notes that First Records was regarded as the best-sounding of Taiwan's pirate labels of the 1960s [@new-jones2020].

His son Yeh Chui-ching 葉垂青 (b. 1952) entered the industry in 1976 as a disc-cutting engineer. In 1977 the family installed Taiwan's only Neumann VMS70 lathe, the first computer-controlled lathe in the country, with a Studer A80 machine and a Studer 169 console, at about NT$20 million [@程明2021聲響技術, pp. 71–72]. Until the CD era he cut 80 to 100 LP masters a month and, by his own account, nearly every LP master cut in Taiwan [@cheng-fieldwork, interviews from 2015]. Tapes arrived with levels and tonal balance varying from song to song; recording engineers treated cutting as a black box [@程明2021聲響技術, pp. 71–72]. He concluded that the problems had to be fixed upstream, in the recording itself, and describes the recording quality of the time as meeting only a low standard [@cheng-fieldwork, interviews from 2015].

### 4.2 Hirata Giichi and the 1978 experimental session

The Yehs met the Japanese engineer Hirata Giichi 平田義一 (d. 2019) at a European audio fair in 1973, where he recommended the Neumann system [@程明2021聲響技術, p. 72; @kuramochi_europe_1973]. Hirata, then at the record division of Toho 東宝, had argued in a 1973 round-table for a "Hi-Fi way" (Hi-Fi 道), a search for the correct sound that joins concept and technique [@sugano_1973_mixing]. The dissertation judges that his importance for Taiwan lies less in his modest Japanese record than in his bridging role: strong English, contacts with European manufacturers, and a value system about what a good recording is [@cheng2026dissertation; @hirata_japan_1979].

Because a better lathe could not repair poor masters, the Yehs took Hirata's advice to build a studio. At the end of 1978 they ran what Yeh calls an experimental recording: 52 Taipei session players assembled through Hsu Chung-hsien 徐崇憲 of Lifeng, Japanese arrangers and engineers brought by Hirata, and a 16-track machine with a Neve console that Doris Brougham's 彭蒙惠 Overseas Radio and Television 救世傳播協會 had obtained for its Yun-te Studio 韻德錄音室 [@程明2021聲響技術, pp. 74–75]. The album Changcheng Yao 長城謠 (First Records FM-6001, 1978) advertised both the largest orchestra then recorded in Taiwan and multitrack recording; it sold well at NT$150, and the Yehs committed about NT$30 million to Platinum, which Hirata reported in 1979 as the island's first 24-track studio, due before year end [@程明2021聲響技術, pp. 74–75; @hirata_japan_1979].

### 4.3 What multitrack changes

A track is one independently recordable, erasable and re-recordable channel on the tape machine. The decisive property is not the channel count but the independence of each track, which lets one part be redone without touching the others, provided bleed is controlled by close miking and by recording in groups [@程明2021聲響技術, pp. 76–77; @horning2013chasing]. Real-time coordination of the ensemble, where balance was set by moving players nearer or farther from the microphones, dissolves: a song can be recorded in sections, at different times, in different studios, and mixed down later [@cheng2026dissertation]. Drummer Huang Jui-feng 黃瑞豐 describes his career as a passage from two-track ping-pong recording to 82 tracks [@pmdb-oralhistory]. The social consequences: players learn to play to a click track and headphones; arrangers plan the song in recordable sections and become process designers; engineers judge single tracks without hearing the ensemble [@cheng2026dissertation; @horning_2004_engineering].

### 4.4 Adoption and standardization, 1976–1980

Multitrack did not arrive with Platinum. Hsu Chung-hsien had bought an 8-track system for Lifeng in 1976; Hirata counted three 16-track studios in Taiwan in 1979; Platinum's 24 tracks extended this base [@cheng2026dissertation; @hirata_japan_1979]. What 1976–1980 fixed was a configuration. Hirata's brief for Platinum (clean, bright rooms; full isolation with an acoustic lock; a lounge, no food in the studio; 24 tracks as the top standard of the day), with acoustic measurement at each construction stage, became the uniform look of Taipei studios in the 1980s, and he eventually designed all 13 of Platinum's rooms [@hirata_1998_baijin; @程明2021聲響技術, p. 81]. Good Sound engineer Yu Chao-yang 游朝陽 recalls that other owners had to upgrade quickly or lose work [@cheng-fieldwork, 2018]. The dissertation calls this closure in the SCOT sense: multitrack was adopted when an arrangement of personnel, tacit knowledge and labour rhythm stabilized around it, and the underlying shift was in the criterion of a good record, from cultural legitimacy (a full orchestra playing together) to efficiency under the dense release schedule of the campus folk-song movement [@Bijker1987FactsAndArtefacts; @cheng2026dissertation].

### 4.5 Why studios, not labels, sit at the center of the 1957–1991 graph

Hirata observed in 1998 that among nearly 200 Taiwanese record and production companies none owned a studio: recording, mixing and mastering were outsourced to commercial studios, which therefore held better equipment than the labels [@hirata_1998_baijin, p. 78]. The dissertation reads this as Théberge's network studio [@theberge2004network]. Two conditions preceded Platinum: Taiwan's film industry had worked in post-synchronization since Lee Hsing's 李行 Oyster Girl 蚵女 (1964), with scores recorded in record companies' studios [@黃東嶽2014李行; @凌紫鈞2018瓊瑤]; and the closure of the US military clubs after January 1979 released a generation of players who became session musicians [@chu2020rnr]. By 1998 Hirata listed nine Taipei studios with 37 rooms [@hirata_1998_baijin, p. 79].

### 4.6 Engineers: scarcity, then diffusion

Wang Chia-tung 王家棟 (b. 1951) recalls that in 1983, before joining Lifeng, he checked LP credits in a Taipei record shop and found Hsu Chung-hsien's name on an estimated nine tenths of them [@pmdb-oralhistory]. From about 1986 work shifted to Platinum, partly because Lifeng's Otari 32-track format was incompatible with other studios [@pmdb-oralhistory; @cheng2026dissertation].

## 5. Chen Chih-Yuan and the segmented arrangement (1978–1991)

*This section grounds the paper's statements: "The single most powerful bridge in the entire 1957–1991 network is the arranger designated as Arr. #1 … Chen Chih-Yuan. Chen first appears in the network as the keyboardist of the military-club cohort, and transitioned around 1978 from instrumentalist to commissioned arranger. By the end of the period, he was active across virtually the entire industry: every major label, studio, and stylistic tradition in the dataset" (4.2) and "the works by Chen Chih-Yuan and his protégé are spread across the entire space, overlapping with every other tradition … the person who crossed all traditions also produced sound that crosses all clusters" (4.3), together with Table 1's observation that arrangers, pop instrumentalists, backing vocalists and producers take over the bridging positions after 1978.*
{: .grounds}

![Figure 2c of the paper: 1957–1991. One giant component. Recording studios sit at the centre with engineers and several arrangers; labels and lyricists are pushed to the edge. Arr. #1 is Chen Chih-Yuan.](assets/img/network_1957_1991.jpg)


### 5.1 Production pressure after 1977

The first author's dissertation (Cheng 2026) treats the arranger's rise as an effect of production volume, not of one career. Hsin-ko Records 新格唱片 held the first Golden Melody Prize 金韻獎 campus contest in June 1977 and released a compilation that December; the series ran to ten volumes by 1981, Haishan 海山 answered with the annual Folk Style 民謠風 series from 1978, and Rock Records 滾石 (1981) and UFO Records 飛碟 (1982) were founded inside the same wave [@張釗維1994誰在那邊; @李明璁2015時代迴音]. Ho Tung-hung 何東洪 and Chang Chao-wei 張釗維 describe this as the institutionalisation of large-scale production in the Mandarin record industry [@何東洪張釗維2000國語唱片]. None of Taipei's nearly two hundred record and production companies owned a studio, so the work went to commercial studios such as Platinum 白金 (opened 1980) and Lifeng Studio 麗風錄音室, which had to keep rooms, engineers and session players busy [@cheng2026dissertation]. Engineer Wang Chia-tung 王家棟 (b. 1951) recalled that in 1983 he and Hsu Chung-hsien 徐崇憲, Lifeng's only two engineers, ran the studio around the clock for two months [@new-tmcia-bandfire36]. The dissertation takes this as proof that pressure alone did not pick a workflow: Lifeng coped by extending hours and keeping synchronous sessions. The segmented workflow won because pressure coincided with a person able to organise caseload, players and studio time, and with a rhythm section able to deliver under separated tracking [@cheng2026dissertation].

### 5.2 The segmented arrangement 分段編曲

The term denotes the workflow of Chen Chih-Yuan 陳志遠 (1950–2011): on receiving a demo he fixed form, style and harmony, handed the rhythm-section parts to the production company for immediate recording, and wrote the strings while that session ran, for recording the next day. Cutting engineer Yeh Chui-ching 葉垂青 summarised it as "even the arrangement can be done in segments" [@程明2021聲響技術]. In big-band synchronous recording the score had to be complete before anyone played; here the arranger's own labour was cut into stages and re-ordered to the availability of rooms, players and producer opinion [@cheng2026dissertation].

The dissertation is explicit that this was not the natural result of multitrack recording. Once 24-track machines had been adopted and standardized at Platinum and other commercial studios, the equipment still admitted two readings: a tool for patching synchronous takes, or a condition for splitting production into stages. The second stabilised between roughly 1977 and 1981 because folk-era volume, the structural need for an arranger, the rhythm section's tacit coordination and the gradual entry of the producer title pushed in the same direction [@cheng2026dissertation].

Its material carrier was Chen's one-page "master score" 總譜, with melody, chord symbols, rhythm pattern and every instrumental line on a single sheet; Wang Chi-chung 王啟仲 treats sight-reading it as the entry threshold for studio work in the late 1980s [@王啟仲2022重探臺灣搖滾樂場景中]. Producer Li Tsung-sheng 李宗盛 (b. 1958) stated that every line was Chen's and that Chen carried the largest arranging volume in the industry, charging NT$4,000 a song while clients queued past midnight [@王淳眉2016製作]. Guitarist Ni Fang-lai 倪方來 recalled players waiting in the studio for the score to be printed, and producer Tsao Chun-hung 曹俊鴻 (b. 1955) remembered Chen writing on Platinum's top floor while strings, rhythm section and vocals were recorded below [@pmdb-oralhistory]. The dissertation calls this a hierarchical allocation rather than a total closure: solos and timbral details were deliberately left to particular players, a "structural blank" 結構性留白 described from the players' side by guitarist Yu Cheng-yen 游正彥 and saxophonist Fan Wang 番王 [@pmdb-oralhistory].

### 5.3 From keyboardist to position-holder

The Gang of Four 四人幫 consisted of Chen (piano and keyboard), drummer Huang Jui-feng 黃瑞豐 (b. 1950), guitarist Yu Cheng-yen (b. 1952) and bassist Kuo Tsung-shao 郭宗韶 (1950–2017); the latter three were leading players of the US military-club band era of the 1960s and early 1970s [@馬世芳2015時空地圖; @chu2020rnr]. The dissertation notes that Chen's path differed slightly: he is less often remembered as a club musician than in university bands, restaurant residencies, television backing and arranging, and his working relationship with the other three was built through hot bands and TV work in the mid-to-late 1970s [@cheng2026dissertation]. In the first author's judgment this is a matter of memory rather than biography: Chen's later role as arranger was so prominent that it eclipsed his years as a band keyboardist, which is why the paper counts him with the military-club cohort. He had played in Weng Ching-hsi's 翁清溪 Tony big band, and Weng introduced him to recording and arranging [@李律2022不褪色的音符]. Ni Fang-lai grouped him with Chen Yu-li 陳玉立 among the "old arrangers" who always wrote scores [@pmdb-oralhistory]. The dissertation's explanation is structural: the keyboard is the one band position that handles harmony, voicing and form at once, so when studios needed someone to fix form, harmony, instrumentation and key before recording, the keyboardist's skill was the easiest to enlarge into the arranger's post [@cheng2026dissertation]. After the US withdrawal of 1979 the clubs closed and the players converged on the studios, a move the dissertation ties to Chen's rapidly expanding caseload of 1979 to 1981 [@cheng2026dissertation].

On this reading the centrality we measure is a structural trace: around 1977 folk-era volume, studio time, rhythm-section players, producer expectations and label caseloads all concentrated on the arranger, and Chen was the person best able to bear that concentration; the dissertation declines to read the network position as evidence of a lone genius [@cheng2026dissertation]. Centralisation was also a concentration of workload. Chen recalled seventy-two hours without sleep; Tu Ying 屠穎 (1963–2025) likened the industry to a sponge squeezing whoever proved useful; Han Hsien-kuang 韓賢光 said at Chen's 2011 memorial that an industry distrustful of newcomers had kept forcing the work onto him [@pmdb-oralhistory].

### 5.4 Producers emerge, lineage continues

The producer 製作人 title was still unfamiliar around 1980: arranger Ho Ching-ching 何慶清 recalled being asked to produce an album without knowing what a producer was [@pmdb-oralhistory]. Li Shou-chuan 李壽全 (b. 1955), who joined Hsin-ko in 1979, asked Chen for a cello on Li Chien-fu's 李建復 (b. 1959) Descendants of the Dragon (1980); Chen later told him it was the first time a producer had instructed him how to arrange [@pmdb-oralhistory]. Rock Records (1981) adopted a flat producer system, UFO (1982) drew clear lines between in-house producers, arrangers and engineers, and by the mid-1980s producer intervention in arrangement had become a convention in Becker's sense [@cheng2026dissertation; @becker2008artworlds]. Chen's formal move into UFO in 1987 internalised the arranger [@cheng2026dissertation].

On sound, engineer Hsu Chung-hsien contrasted Chen's generation, who added strings, with the brass-based arrangers of the television big bands [@pmdb-oralhistory]. Transmission was indirect: Tu Ying learned by watching Chen and by listening to Chen's Platinum multitracks track by track under engineer Lin Ming-yang 林明陽; Tu Hui-yuan 凃惠源 gave Chen a book of synthesizer patches that Chen then used [@pmdb-oralhistory]. Tu Ying counted the roughly ten MIDI-era arrangers of his cohort plus Chen as the source of some three hundred albums [@pmdb-oralhistory].

## 6. Four arrangement traditions

*This section grounds the paper's definition: "we defined four arrangement traditions: (1) the Pre-multitrack tradition, before the mid-1970s; (2) the Chen Chih-Yuan tradition, covering Chen and his protégé; (3) the Electone tradition, a parallel path rooted in the electronic organ, serving primarily the Taiwanese-language market under tighter budget constraints; and (4) the Post-Chen era, styles from the late 1980s onward." We also wrote that "the Pre-multitrack tradition, the Electone tradition, and the Post-Chen era each occupy distinct regions of the embedding space."*
{: .grounds}

![Figure 3b of the paper: UMAP projection of MERT embeddings of vocal-separated recordings, coloured by arrangement tradition. Each point is a recording.](assets/img/mert_traditions.png)


### 6.1 Pre-multitrack tradition

This group covers arrangers who worked before multitrack recording was adopted and standardized in Taiwan's studios, when a band recorded together and the arranger's work was coupled to conducting and live orchestration. The MERT sample represents it with Lin Li-han 林禮涵, Hsu Shih 許石 and Weng Ching-hsi 翁清溪, the big-band arrangers presented in Section 1 [@cheng2026dissertation]. The dissertation places the acoustic break at 1977–1978 and the institutional one at 1979, when Platinum Studio 白金錄音室 opened (see Section 4).

### 6.2 Chen Chih-Yuan tradition

This group is the Mandarin core arranging position at the moment of standardized closure around 1979, represented by Chen Chih-Yuan 陳志遠 (1950–2011) and his protégé Chen Yu-li 陳玉立, the keyboardist who stood in for Chen when he was overbooked and whom studio insiders counted among the core session players [@cheng2026dissertation]. “Protégé” is used here in the loose sense the Taiwanese trade gives it: a younger musician who takes over some of a senior's cases is spoken of as that senior's student, and the senior is addressed as 老師 (teacher), without any formal apprenticeship. The first author (Cheng 2026) anchors the whole grouping on Chen: before him, his core, the parallel path, and after him [@cheng2026dissertation]. The KB lineage, Chen's working routine and the "position, not person" argument are covered in Section 5.

### 6.3 Electone tradition

The paper's "Electone" (Yamaha electronic organ) label corresponds to the dissertation's 臺語電子琴編曲圈, the Taiwanese-language electronic-organ arranging circle. The dissertation defines it by production conditions and position rather than by language alone: lower production cost, the electronic organ as the lead instrument, and a working milieu separate from the Mandarin core studios [@cheng2026dissertation]. The MERT sample uses Lin Kuo-hsiung 林國雄, Liu Ching-chih 劉清池 (b. 1943) and Chi Ming-yang 紀明陽; the dissertation's case study is Liu [@cheng2026dissertation].

Liu's career explains why the dissertation calls this a parallel path rather than a margin. He entered the studio at twenty-three as a guitarist recording instrumental albums for Ling Ling Records 鈴鈴唱片, then joined the band of the Taiwanese-language arranger Lin Li-han, learning arrangement from the band's playing styles and timbres before formally arranging a Teresa Teng 鄧麗君 album [@new-linmiaozi2024]. He originally arranged in both languages; his specialization came when the industry split into Taiwanese and Mandarin camps after the campus folk-song wave and Lin Li-han handed the Taiwanese-language work to him [@new-linmiaozi2024]. Chen Pei-feng 陳培豐, whose study the dissertation builds on, lists work on both sides, from early Teresa Teng songs for the Mandarin market to Yeh Chi-tien 葉啟田 (b. 1948)'s "Love to Fight, Then You Will Win" 愛拚才會贏 for the Taiwanese market [@chen2022decolonization]. Chen also states that by number of songs arranged Liu ranks first in Taiwanese popular music history, and that his guitar and electronic-organ light-music records outsold the catalogues of the era's biggest stars [@chen2022decolonization].

The sound has a name in the trade. Keyboards carrying MIDI sound sources (MIDI, the 1983 standard for connecting electronic instruments) were called 魔音琴 ("magic-sound keyboard") in Taiwanese-language arranging circles from the late 1980s into the early 1990s; the Mandarin studios said "synthesizer" or "electronic organ" [@cheng2026dissertation]. The dissertation reads the naming as an interpretive stance: the same MIDI, synthesizers and samplers that the Mandarin core absorbed into a centralized workflow were here turned toward a sound described as 俗擱有力 (Taiwanese: "vulgar yet powerful") [@cheng2026dissertation]. Chen Pei-feng ties this to what he calls the "double decolonization" of the 1980s, the lifting of martial law and the establishment of copyright law, which cut Taiwanese-language song off from Japanese cover versions; Liu, in Chen's words, "dressed [the songs] in the posture of 俗擱有力" so that "purely made-in-Taiwan" Taiwanese-language songs could circulate in quantity [@chen2022decolonization]. Liu's own position, as quoted by Chen, rejects the hierarchy behind the "vulgar" label: music comes before music theory, and its value lies in its connection to people [@chen2022decolonization].

For the paper's figures, the relevant point is that this path was never absorbed by the Mandarin core and kept its own working logic. The dissertation anticipates a separate region in the sound space, and the UMAP projections indicate one, with and without Chen Chih-Yuan's works in the input [@cheng2026dissertation].

### 6.4 Post-Chen era

This group holds arrangers who entered the Mandarin core arranging position only after the Chen Chih-Yuan model had closed around it. The MERT sample consists of five arrangers chosen for balance, not as an exhaustive roster: Tu Hui-yuan 凃惠源, Tu Ying 屠穎, Wang Yu-min 王豫民, Huang Yun-ling 黃韻玲 and Chung Hsing-min 鍾興民 [@cheng2026dissertation]. "Post-Chen" is the dissertation's analytical label; the cohort's own name for itself is "MIDI Boy" [@cheng2026dissertation].

The cohort continues the KB lineage (keyboard-trained arrangers) of Section 5. Tu Ying, in his oral history, identifies himself as a keyboardist and places Chen as the predecessor of "our generation"; Tu Hui-yuan traces his training from piano through electronic organ to the Roland Jupiter 4 and 8 synthesizers [@pmdb-oralhistory]. Ho Chia-na 何佳娜's interviews with five arrangers active from the 1990s, Chung Hsing-min among them, likewise find keyboard backgrounds throughout [@何佳娜2017默會]. The dissertation therefore treats the "old arrangers" of the 1980s and the MIDI Boys of the 1990s as one lineage under two sets of technical conditions, with MIDI as the second cohort's main working interface [@cheng2026dissertation].

What changed was access. Tu Ying recalls that roughly ten younger arrangers, himself and Tu Hui-yuan among them, got their chance only because of MIDI, and that these ten plus Chen account for the bulk of the period's output; the circle widened slightly but stayed dense [@pmdb-oralhistory]. Entry to Chen's own practice was uneven. Tu Hui-yuan compiled a notebook of two to three hundred patches he had programmed on the Jupiter 4 and gave it to Chen at Lifeng Studio 麗風錄音室, later recognizing his own timbres on Chen's records; Tu Ying learned Chen's arrangements by listening to Platinum Studio multitrack masters one track at a time with the engineer Lin Ming-yang 林明陽 [@pmdb-oralhistory]. The dissertation summarizes the pattern as redistribution: people were kept out of the central position, sound resources were absorbed, and knowledge travelled indirectly through engineers, masters and studios [@cheng2026dissertation].

The collaboration network dates the transition. In the 1982–1986 window Chen ranks first by centrality and by Burt's constraint, ahead of Platinum and Lifeng studios; in 1987–1991 he drops to 18th, because he had joined UFO Records 飛碟唱片 as an in-house producer and, by his own account, generally declined work from other labels [@pmdb-oralhistory]. The brokerage positions passed to studios (Platinum and Yaxian 雅絃, nearly tied), to backing vocalists, session players and engineers, and to Rock Records 滾石唱片; Tu Hui-yuan enters the top ten brokers in this window, though as a supplier of timbres and new tools inside the existing structure, not as the head of a separate system [@cheng2026dissertation].

The MERT projections suggest that this cohort's works occupy a region distinct from both the pre-multitrack and Electone groups while overlapping with Chen's, and that removing Chen's works sharpens the separation [@cheng2026dissertation]. The dissertation reads this only as an independent reference alongside the network and oral evidence, not as succession or departure.

## 7. Sources and how to read them

**The dissertation.** Cheng (2026) is a history of the recording studio as a social institution, written from the position of a working session guitarist, and framed by the Social Construction of Technology and by Antoine Hennion's sociology of amateurs [@cheng2026dissertation]. Chapters 3 to 5 cover the periods treated on this page; chapter 6 continues into the 1990s. It is written in Chinese; the English summary in the paper is ours.

**Oral histories.** Most first-person testimony comes from the *Oral History of Senior Musicians* collected since 2010 by the Taipei Music Center's Taiwan Popular Music Database [@pmdb-oralhistory], more than a hundred filmed interviews with performers, songwriters, arrangers, session players, engineers and label owners. The dissertation reads these recordings as sources on working practice rather than as biography, and cross-checks them against each other and against the press. On this page they are cited collectively; the interviewee is named in the sentence. Where the dissertation draws on the first author's own field interviews (2015–2020), they are cited as [@cheng-fieldwork].

**Newspapers and trade magazines.** The *United Daily News* 聯合報 and the *Economic Daily News* 經濟日報 supply dated evidence for the 1950s and 1960s; the 1968 special issue of *Today Pictorial* 今天畫刊 is the only contemporary survey of the band scene written by its members. Japanese audio-engineering journals (*Hōsō Gijutsu* 放送技術, *Rajio Gijutsu* ラジオ技術) and *Studio Sound* record Hirata Giichi's reports on Taiwan's studios.

**Scholarship.** For the Taiwanese-language record industry, Huang Yu-yuan [@黃裕元2016歌唱王國] and Chen Pei-feng [@chen2017rethinking; @chen2022decolonization]; for the military clubs, Chan Meng-tung [@詹孟桐2020美軍], Shin and Ho [@shin_ho_2009_translation] and Chu [@chu2020rnr]; for arrangers' tacit knowledge, Ho Chia-na [@何佳娜2017默會]; for the studio as a technology, Horning [@horning2013chasing] and Théberge [@theberge2004network]. Full details are in the reference list below.

**What this page does not do.** It does not reproduce the dissertation's argument in full, it does not quote interview transcripts, and it does not settle the questions the dissertation leaves open (noted in the paper's Limitations). Readers who want the primary materials are welcome to write to us.
