Digital Existence as Ontological Intervention

Backstage Networks in Postwar Taiwanese Popular Music

Ming Cheng · Yung-Chuan Chang · Chien-Chang Yang · Li Su

Institute of Information Science, Academia Sinica · Graduate Institute of Musicology, National Taiwan University · Taipei, Taiwan

On a Taiwanese pop record from the 1960s, 70s or 80s, the singer's name and face are on the front of the sleeve. The people who made the sound are on the back, in small print, if at all: the arranger who wrote the parts, the session musicians who played them, the engineer who recorded them. Histories of Taiwanese pop are told through its singers. The names on the back were left out, and their credits have never been gathered into a dataset.

PASMA (the Private Archive of Scanned Music Artifacts) is built from that small print: the liner notes of 1,122 Taiwanese vinyl records released between 1957 and 1991. We transcribed who worked on each record and in what role, linked them into a network of who worked with whom, and compared that network with the sound of the recordings themselves. For these workers, being entered into a dataset is the first time they exist as data at all. That is what the title of our ISMIR 2026 paper means by digital existence.

What we ask

  1. Who actually makes the music? Our previous study of the Taiwanese recording studio 1 argues that the backstage, not the star, is where popular music is made.1 The singer signs the sound; the arrangers, players and engineers produce it. The paper tests this on the collaboration network: in no period do singers appear among the top 30 bridging actors (Table 1). The connective tissue of the industry is arrangers, session players, backing vocalists and engineers.
  2. Do people who work together make music that sounds alike? The same study proposes that backstage workers form relevant social groups around shared taste and working conventions, and that such a group leaves a recognizable mark on the sound. The paper tests this with MERT embeddings of vocal-separated recordings: the four arrangement traditions defined by network communities occupy distinct regions of the acoustic space, and the one arranger who crossed every tradition, CHEN Chih-yuan 陳志遠,2 produced sound that crosses every cluster; remove his works and the remaining traditions separate more sharply (Fig. 3). The embeddings indicate, rather than prove, that social ties and sound are entangled.
  3. What can computation and history do for each other? Computation does what reading the archive cannot: it dates the structural threshold to a two-year window and shows that a social position has an acoustic counterpart. History does what computation cannot: it makes 1978 not a kink in a time series but the industrial adoption and standardization of multitrack recording, with studios, people and policies attached. Each side changes what the other is entitled to claim, and MIR gains a purpose its usual applications do not anticipate: a historiographic one.

A reply to Born

  1. Georgina Born's call for a diversified and critical MIR 2 makes three points the paper takes up. The field's datasets and benchmarks encode assumptions about what music is, who makes it, and which aspects of music-making merit representation. Reducing music to computational knowledge representations risks conflating epistemology with ontology, flattening its embodied and social existence into data. The remedy is agonistic interdisciplinarity: computational and humanistic disciplines engaging as equals, each able to transform the other's assumptions.
  2. Our reply: we change all three assumptions at once (a non-Western historical repertoire, the backstage rather than the star, collaboration ties and backstage sound rather than consumer-facing attributes). On the warning about reduction we argue for a reversal: for these workers the condition is not reduction but absence, and in an age of metadata absence from the record is erasure from history. Being inscribed into the very infrastructure Born criticizes is what grants them digital existence. The act is an ontological intervention, not a representation. And the study is built as an instance of the remedy, which is point 3 above.

What the evidence shows

  1. Two methods that never met point to the same year. From the credits, three network properties that need not move together turn in the same window, 1976–1978. From the recordings, given no dates, MERT embeddings split at 1978. Their convergence indicates that the social reorganization of backstage labour left measurable traces in the sound. What that year was, and who was behind it, is told on the Historical Context page.
  2. Archival silence is itself evidence. Early liner notes do not credit technical staff; the paper reads this not as a documentation gap but as a condition: engineers and producers had not yet accumulated the professional standing to be named. The dense formalization of credits after 1978 is the moment the backstage consolidated enough power to claim recognition, closure in the sense of the Social Construction of Technology 3.
  3. The encoding is not innocent, and we say so. 15 role categories are a reduction of hundreds of printed credit labels; an edge is a reduction of a working relationship; an embedding is a reduction of a performance. For the backstage this reduction is the price of existence, and it is worth paying when the alternative is not a richer ontology but no entry at all.

Access to the data

PASMA is reconstructed from a private collection, and the scans are shared with us on the collector's terms. The dataset is therefore not publicly downloadable. Researchers who need the credit data for non-commercial research are welcome to write to us; see the Dataset page for what we can share and how.

Scans: private collection of Pei-Ju Lin, founder of the Taipei music space Sounds Good. Portraits on the People page: Taipei Music Center, Taiwan Popular Music Database. Supported by the Academia Sinica project “Innovative Applications of AI in Humanities and Sciences” (AS-IAIA-114-M06).

  1. The paper says backstage; the Chinese term for this position is muhou 幕後. The word is not a researcher's coinage. The workers took it for themselves when a magazine addressed to them was launched under that title in 1983, with Behind the Scene as its own English name. The first author's dissertation 1 keeps muhou as an analytical term rather than translating it away, on the ground that in the usual division of scholarly labour concepts travel outward from the theoretical centre while experiences from elsewhere arrive as cases, and an italicized local word is too easily read as colour awaiting translation into theory. This site keeps the paper's English for consistency with the paper, but we record the point here because it belongs to the same argument as Born's call for a diversified MIR: a field that decides who counts as making music also decides in whose words that making is described. ↩

  2. Chinese and Japanese personal names are written surname first, as they are spoken, with the surname in capitals and the characters at first mention: CHEN Chih-yuan 陳志遠. Quotations from the paper and its own labels, such as the Chen Chih-Yuan tradition, keep the paper's spelling. ↩
